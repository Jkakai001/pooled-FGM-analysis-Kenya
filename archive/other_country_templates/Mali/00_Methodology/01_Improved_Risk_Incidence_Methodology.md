# Improved Methodology for FGM Risk Incidence and Attitudinal Risk Incidence

Scope: DHS and MICS based risk incidence generation for 18 countries across multiple survey years


# Purpose

We Review the current  UNFPA FGM incidence risk methodology  and build on it to introduce a new  methodology drawn from  peer reviewed literature and established demographic small area estimation tooling. 

# 1. What the current methodology does well, and where it can be strengthened

The existing approach, a cohort based time to event analysis of retrospective age at cutting data from DHS and MICS, is the statistically correct starting point. Peer reviewed literature confirms this is the current standard technique for FGM timing analysis (see the 2025 PLOS One study of age at circumcision in Ethiopia, and the frailty model comparison study also using Ethiopian DHS data). The value of treating FGM as a survival outcome rather than a simple prevalence proportion is well established, since most cutting happens very early in life and a cross sectional prevalence figure among women 15 to 49 understates recent incidence.

The following limitations can be sighted from the current implementation:

First, the method currently produces separate Kaplan Meier curves stratified by birth cohort band and by urban or rural residence, read qualitatively as declining or not declining. This is descriptive rather than inferential, there is no formal statistical test or credible interval attached to the claim that a country's incidence is or is not declining, and no way to compare the strength of a trend across countries on a common scale.

Second, the subnational maps shown for Ethiopia, Kenya and Somalia appear to be direct tabulations by DHS survey domain rather than a modeled, uncertainty aware surface. Domains with few observed cases will have very noisy direct estimates, and adjoining domains that plausibly share risk levels are not able to borrow strength from each other.

Third, the method currently relies on DHS and MICS alone, a limitation the deck's own final slides acknowledge and flag as a future direction, drawing an explicit analogy to the multi source triangulation used in United Nations Inter agency Group for Child Mortality Estimation (UN IGME) under five mortality estimation.

Fourth, the deck's own methodology table only covers ten countries through 2020, several years out of date relative to the current 18 country requirement, and the "attitudinal risk incidence" half of the task has no visible methodology at all in this file.

# 2. Recommended methodology, in five parts

## 2.1 Replace stratified Kaplan Meier with a formal discrete time hazard regression

Rather than fitting separate survival curves per cohort band and eyeballing the difference, restructure each woman's or girl's retrospective record into person year (or person month) format, the same transformation used in demographic mortality analysis, and fit a discrete time hazard model, essentially a logistic regression of the event indicator (cut in this year of life or not, conditional on not yet cut) on age, birth cohort or calendar year, region, residence, and any other covariates of interest, with survey weights and clustering by primary sampling unit applied throughout.

This single model replaces the current set of separate curves with one that produces a formally estimated cohort trend, a standard error and credible interval for that trend, and covariate effects that are directly comparable across countries. Two literature backed refinements to this core model are worth adopting together.

Add a shared frailty term for cluster or region, an unobserved random effect capturing the fact that girls in the same community share cultural and social exposures the model cannot otherwise measure. A parametric frailty comparison using Ethiopian DHS data found that a log logistic baseline hazard with an inverse Gaussian frailty term fit best, and that regional clustering was a statistically significant, non ignorable feature of the data. Fitting a model without this term risks overstating precision.

Where the goal is a birth cohort trend rather than a fully parametric hazard shape, use a Bayesian penalized spline (P spline) or a second order random walk prior on the cohort or period effect rather than a low flexibility parametric curve. This is the same smoothing device UN IGME uses for its Bayesian B spline bias reduction (B3) model for under five mortality, described further below, and it avoids both overfitting to noisy single year cohorts and underfitting a genuinely nonlinear decline.

## 2.2 Adapt an existing subnational small area hazard framework rather than building one from scratch

The most actionable recommendation in this document is this one. The R package SUMMER, developed for and actively used in UN IGME's own subnational under five mortality estimates, already implements the exact architecture the current FGM deck gestures toward when it draws the under five mortality analogy. SUMMER converts birth history data into person month format, computes design based direct estimates by region and period using Horvitz Thompson estimation, then applies Bayesian spatial and temporal smoothing through the INLA framework, with a spatial adjacency (Markov random field) structure over admin regions and a random walk prior over time, and can further adjust the smoothed estimates against external benchmarks.

Every one of these components is outcome agnostic. The only change required to repurpose SUMMER for FGM incidence risk is to substitute the event definition, from a death within an age interval to a first cutting within an age interval, everything downstream, the person month construction, the direct estimation, the spatial and temporal smoothing, and the credible interval extraction, carries over. This converts a multi year methods development exercise into an adaptation exercise, and it produces subnational estimates for domains with sparse data by construction, borrowing strength from neighbouring regions and from adjacent time periods, precisely the sparse data problem that a companion academic study (Weny and colleagues, cross border clustering in Ethiopia, Kenya and Somalia, Studies in Family Planning 2023) frames explicitly as the central methodological challenge for this region.

A close academic precedent already exists and is worth reading before building anything: a Bayesian hierarchical spatiotemporal model was published evaluating FGM and cutting prevalence trends among girls aged 0 to 14 in Nigeria, pooling six survey rounds across DHS and MICS from 2003 to 2016 and 2017, using structured additive regression (fit in R2BayesX) with separate structured and unstructured spatial effects, a second order random walk for time, and a fixed effect adjusting for survey type. It found no significant difference between DHS and MICS methodology once that adjustment was included, useful reassurance for a pipeline that must pool both survey types across 18 countries. A closely related Bayesian hierarchical spatial and spatiotemporal analysis was also published for Kenya alone, using Kenyan DHS rounds from 1998 to 2014. Both are proof that this class of model is already accepted in the FGM literature specifically, not only in the child mortality literature it is borrowed from.

## 2.3 Formalize multi source triangulation the way UN IGME does, rather than informally

The deck's own future direction slide calls for moving beyond DHS and MICS toward census and administrative data such as health information systems, explicitly by analogy to how under five mortality triangulates vital registration, full birth history surveys, summary birth history surveys and household death recall. The UN IGME B3 model offers a directly reusable template for how to do this rigorously rather than by ad hoc averaging. In the B3 model each data source type carries its own bias parameter, estimated hierarchically across all series of that type, so that a systematically biased source can still contribute trend information without distorting the estimated level, and country specific smoothness is estimated with partial pooling across countries so that small countries borrow strength on how much year to year fluctuation is plausible without having their actual trend forced toward the global average.

For FGM incidence risk this suggests a concrete recommendation, in the near term add a source type fixed effect for DHS versus MICS as the Nigeria study did, and in the medium term, once JP FGM administrative data, health information system records (DHIS2), or the DIOS social media signal are validated as measuring something related to incidence, add each as its own source type with its own bias term rather than merging them into the survey based model as if they were additional surveys of the same quality.

## 2.4 Upgrade the visualization from separate charts to a Lexis surface heatmap

The current deck communicates cohort trends as several separate Kaplan Meier line charts per country, one visual per cohort band, which makes it hard to see age effects, period effects and cohort effects together, and hard to compare countries at a glance. Demography has a purpose built visualization for exactly this problem, the Lexis diagram, drawn as a heatmap with age on one axis and calendar time on the other, cutting risk (or cumulative probability of having been cut) mapped to color, and birth cohorts appearing as diagonal bands across the surface. A single Lexis surface per country shows the same information as the current five or six separate cohort curves, plus period effects the current charts cannot show at all, such as a specific survey year in which cutting practices shifted for all ages simultaneously, for example due to a change in law or a program intervention.

For a case where perceptual distortion at the edges of a rotated Lexis surface is a concern, the literature also documents an alternative called a hexamap, which remaps the same age period cohort information onto a hexagonal grid to avoid the visual distortion that occurs at the boundary of a standard rotated Lexis surface, implemented in R.

A faceted grid of Lexis heatmaps, one small panel per country, arranged together, would give a single overview graphic for all 18 countries at once, directly useful for the kind of report your DOA calls for, and considerably more information dense than the current country by country slide format.

## 2.5 Add a machine learning layer for covariate importance and cross checking

This is a natural extension of the approach you have already applied on the SAE prevalence side, where random forest variable importance and collinearity pruning selected covariates for the geospatial regression model. The same logic applies here. Random survival forests and gradient boosted survival models (available in the Python scikit survival library, and in gradient boosted survival form through libraries built on XGBoost) extend that same random forest logic to a time to event outcome, ranking covariates by their contribution to predicting age at cutting without requiring you to specify a parametric hazard shape in advance, and without assuming the effect of any covariate is linear or additive.

Two uses follow directly from this. First, as a covariate screening step feeding into the discrete time hazard or spatial hazard model described above, exactly analogous to the SAE Method 2 workflow you already use, so that the final regression model is built on a defensible, data driven shortlist of predictors rather than a hand picked list. Second, as an independent cross check, if a random survival forest and a Bayesian spatial hazard model produce materially different predicted risk surfaces for the same country, that discrepancy is itself a useful diagnostic worth investigating before either estimate is published. This also directly serves the DOA task of linking geospatial JP programmatic data and Google embeddings into the FGM risk system, since tree based survival methods handle large, heterogeneous, high dimensional covariate sets, exactly what a merged geospatial and embeddings feature set would look like, far more naturally than a hand specified spline regression.

# 3. Add formal change point detection to identify when decline began

The current deck's classification of countries into "recent declines observed" versus "no measurable reduction" is a qualitative read of a chart. A formal Bayesian change point or structural break detection method, applied to each country's smoothed cohort or period trend, would instead return an estimated year (or birth cohort) at which the trend changed, together with a probability or credible interval around that estimate. This converts a descriptive claim into a testable, citable one, and it directly supports program evaluation questions your Joint Programme stakeholders are likely to ask, for example whether an estimated change point aligns with the timing of a legal reform or an intervention rollout in that country. Multiple R packages implement this cleanly for a posterior trend already produced by the hazard model above, so this step adds relatively little additional modeling burden once section 2.1 is in place.

# 4. Data pipeline recommendations for 18 countries across multiple years

The DHS Program's public API (the STATcompiler based DHS API) only exposes already aggregated indicators, roughly 1,500 of them across 90 countries, and does not appear to expose individual level FGM records, so it is not sufficient on its own for a survival analysis that needs each woman's or girl's own age at cutting. The individual recode microdata, the file that actually contains age at cutting, region, and cluster identifiers, must be obtained through a registered DHS Program data request, most efficiently managed programmatically with the rdhs R package, which authenticates against your DHS Program account, lists every available survey and file type per country, and downloads and caches the requested individual recode files, letting you script a single reproducible pull across all 18 countries and every available survey round rather than downloading files by hand from the website. DHS cluster GPS coordinates are a separate download from the DHS Spatial Data Repository and must be linked in afterward, exactly as you are already doing for the Method 2 SAE work.

MICS has no equivalent aggregate indicator API and no equivalent single package found in this review, microdata must be requested and downloaded per country per round directly through the UNICEF MICS data portal. Since your 18 country pipeline will mix DHS and MICS coverage the same way the Nigeria study above did, plan for two separate extraction and harmonization scripts feeding into one common person year format, with the survey type recorded explicitly as a variable at ingestion so it is available for the source type fixed effect recommended in section 2.3.

# 5. What remains genuinely open

The "attitudinal risk incidence" half of your DOA task has no visible methodology anywhere reviewed for this document, this file included. It is worth asking Dr Ahmed or Romesh Silva directly whether a companion methodology exists internally, or whether this is intended to be built from the DHS or MICS attitude module (the standard question on personal support for FGM continuation) using an analogous framework, most plausibly a multilevel logistic or Poisson regression of stated attitude on the same individual and community covariates used in the Frontiers 14 country pooled analysis referenced below, rather than a survival model, since attitude is not naturally a time to event outcome. This is a modeling design decision best confirmed before building rather than assumed.

Once that base question is resolved, if attitudinal incidence turns out to be a proportion style outcome, for example the share of respondents in a region who support continuation, the small area estimation logic already used for prevalence and recommended above for risk incidence would apply directly, since a binary attitude response with region and cluster level covariates is the same statistical problem as SAE prevalence, a unit level or area level model with spatial smoothing over admin regions, not a hazard model. In that case the fastest path is not a third separate pipeline, it is reusing the same DHS or MICS covariate extraction, the same cluster GPS linkage, and the same spatial smoothing infrastructure already being built for Method 2 prevalence and for the SUMMER based risk incidence work in section 2.2, with the outcome variable swapped for the attitude indicator. This would let all three products, subnational FGM prevalence, subnational FGM risk incidence, and subnational attitudinal risk incidence, share one common spatial modeling backbone across the 18 countries rather than three unrelated approaches, and would be worth proposing to Dr Ahmed as the default unless a different attitudinal methodology already exists internally.

# 6. Suggested phased plan

Phase one, reimplement the current ten to eighteen country analysis as a formal discrete time hazard model with cohort P splines and cluster level frailty, replacing the current stratified Kaplan Meier approach, on already available DHS and MICS individual recode data. This alone would let you report credible intervals and a formally estimated trend rather than a qualitative read.

Phase two, stand up an adapted SUMMER pipeline for subnational estimation, starting with one or two countries where DHS cluster GPS and adjacency shapefiles are already in hand from your Method 2 SAE work, then extend to the remaining countries.

Phase three, build the Lexis heatmap visualization layer and the change point detection layer on top of the phase one model outputs, both are relatively lightweight once the underlying hazard model exists.

Phase four, add the random survival forest or gradient boosted survival cross check and covariate screening step, and begin the source type bias modeling described in section 2.3 once JP programmatic or DIOS data are validated as usable inputs.

Phase five, resolve the attitudinal risk incidence methodology question with your supervisor and, once resolved, apply the equivalent covariate and pooling logic from the prevalence and incidence work rather than starting that design from zero.
